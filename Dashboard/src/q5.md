# Q5: Do small airports follow different trends than major hubs?

**Research question:** Do small airports follow different trends than major hubs, and has traffic concentrated at the busiest airports?

The main chart follows one number over time, each airport group's share of all U.S. domestic passengers, from 2015 to 2025. It draws the change since 2015, because the shares move by only a point or two and would otherwise look like flat lines.

<div class="note" label="Definitions">

- **Airport groups** use the FAA hub thresholds on each airport's share of that year's national passengers:
  - **Large hub:** 1% or more.
  - **Medium hub:** 0.25% to under 1%.
  - **Small & non-hub:** under 0.25% (the FAA's small hubs and non-hubs, merged). Called "small airports" below.
- **Top 10:** the ten airports with the most passengers. All ten are large hubs, so this line overlaps the large-hub line. It is not a fourth group.
- **Passengers:** T-100 passengers departing each airport. A connecting traveler counts at every airport they fly out of. The FAA uses enplanements, so our groups are close to the official ones but not identical.
- **Source:** BTS T-100 Domestic Segment, scheduled passenger service (Class F), 2015-2025.

</div>

## Which airports changed group?

```js
const moveYearInput = Inputs.range([2016, 2025], {label: "Compare 2015 with", step: 1, value: 2025});
display(moveYearInput);
const moveYear = Generators.input(moveYearInput);
```

```js
const wrap = document.createElement("div");
wrap.className = "viz-scroll-x";
wrap.append(membershipTable(airportYears, moveYear));
display(wrap);
const t15 = new Map(airportYears.filter((d) => d.year === 2015).map((d) => [d.airport_id, d.tier]));
const moved = airportYears.filter((d) => d.year === moveYear && t15.has(d.airport_id) && t15.get(d.airport_id) !== d.tier)
  .sort((a, b) => d3.ascending(t15.get(a.airport_id), t15.get(b.airport_id)) || d3.descending(a.passengers, b.passengers));
display(tableView(`List: the ${moved.length} airports in a different group in ${moveYear} than in 2015`, moved, [
  {label: "Airport", value: (d) => d.airport},
  {label: "City", value: (d) => d.city},
  {label: "2015 group", value: (d) => t15.get(d.airport_id)},
  {label: `${moveYear} group`, value: (d) => d.tier},
  {label: `${moveYear} share`, value: (d) => `${d3.format(".2f")(d.share_of_national_pct)}%`}
]));
```

<p class="viz-note">Rows are each airport's group in 2015, columns its group in the selected year; the diagonal is airports that stayed put. "No passengers" covers airports with no scheduled passenger departures in one of the two years.</p>

## What does this visualization show?

- **With groups re-assigned every year, the shares barely move.** Large hubs carried 68.5% of passengers in 2015 and 68.6% in 2025. The only visible shift is 2020, when large hubs briefly lost 1.5 points.
- **Following the same airports shows real change.** With groups fixed at 2015, the 2015 large hubs fell to 64.8% of passengers (-3.7 points). The 2015 small airports gained the most (+2.5 points).
- **The two views differ because growing airports moved up a group.** Nashville, Austin, Honolulu and Washington Dulles became large hubs. Nine small airports, including Charleston and Sarasota, became medium hubs.
- **Traffic did not concentrate at the very top.** The ten busiest airports' share fell from 36.0% to 34.8%.
- **So yes, smaller airports grew faster than the big hubs,** but the growth is spread over many airports, and you only see it when you track a fixed set of airports.

Passenger segments count connecting travelers at each airport they depart from, so hub airports are larger here than their local markets.

## How the data was prepared

`pipeline/prep_q4_q5.py` sums Class F passengers per departing airport and year (from the notebook's `shared_airport_year_F.csv`), computes each airport's share of that year's national total, assigns the group for every year, and ranks airports within each year.
